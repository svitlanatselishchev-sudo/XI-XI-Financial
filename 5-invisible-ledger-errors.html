5 financial record errors that can cost your business thousands

Key takeaway: Ledger errors compound. A gap left unresolved for one quarter can take months to unwind later.

A ledger rarely breaks all at once. It drifts — a few dollars here, a missed entry there — until, three months later, the number on your screen and the number in your bank account have quietly stopped agreeing. The five errors below are the ones we see most often, and every one of them stays invisible until someone matches the ledger to the bank statement, line by line.

1. The same bill paid twice

When a bill is entered once from an emailed invoice, and again when the bank feed pulls in the matching payment, most accounting software won't catch the duplicate — it just books two expenses instead of one. A handful of these over a year can overstate your expenses by thousands.

2. Bank fees that never make it into the books

Monthly maintenance fees, wire fees, and card-processing charges often come straight out of your bank account without ever being entered into the ledger. Your bank balance and your book balance start disagreeing by small amounts that add up over time.

3. Payments recorded on the wrong date

A customer payment recorded on the invoice date, instead of the day it actually hit your bank account, will make a month's revenue look right on its own — but wrong the moment you try to match it to that month's bank statement.

4. Transfers between your own accounts booked as income or expense

Moving money between your own accounts — checking to savings, or between two related companies — should never show up as revenue or a cost. When a transfer gets miscoded that way, it makes the business look better or worse than it actually is.

5. Old checks nobody's cleared out

A check that was written but never cashed sits in your ledger as an open item indefinitely. Left alone, these pile up and make your "cash available" number meaningfully different from what's actually in the account.

Why small errors turn into big ones

None of these five is dramatic by itself. The problem is time: the longer an error sits, the more transactions get layered on top of it, and the harder it gets to tell which later entries were correct and which were just working around the original mistake. A gap that would've taken twenty minutes to trace in month one can take days to untangle by month four.

A quick self-check

  • Pull last month's bank statement and your ledger side by side — do the ending balances match, to the penny?
  • Search your vendor payments for any bill amount that shows up twice in the same month.
  • Confirm every bank fee on the statement has a matching line in the ledger.
  • Check that transfers between your own accounts are coded as transfers, not income or expense.
  • List any checks still marked "outstanding" for more than 60 days and confirm they're still valid.

If any of these turns up a gap you can't explain right away, that's usually the moment to bring in a second set of eyes before it compounds any further.
